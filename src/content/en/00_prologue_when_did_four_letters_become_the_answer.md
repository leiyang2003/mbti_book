# Prologue: When Did Four Letters Become the Answer?

I first encountered MBTI while working as a management consultant at McKinsey, during an internal training session.

At that time, I felt surrounded by people competing with me to become future business leaders. The four-letter result I received was ENTJ.

I was thrilled by that result.

"Good. I am an ENTJ."

At the time, I believed that ENTJs were the type most suited for leadership. That was not a fact guaranteed by MBTI; it was a story I read into those four letters. However, at the time, I could not distinguish between the two.

I came to like myself as an ENTJ and sought to protect that image. I thought that if I carefully considered things, made quick decisions, stepped forward, and showed the right answers, then behaving "as myself" would make me a good leader.

Later, I realized that this conviction not only helped me but also confined me.

## Doing the Right Thing Is Not the Same as Being Heard

This happened after I left McKinsey and started working in venture capital investment.

When I disagreed with a partner, I explained my thoughts one after another. If I was right, I didn't need to consider how the other person felt. I thought that if the logic wasn't sufficient, it wouldn't be understood, so I added more words.

However, the more I spoke, the more defensive the other person became. Even after I finished speaking, he had barely taken in my words.

Even though I was trying to say the right thing, communication was not being established.

I began to focus more on listening than speaking. I tried to understand what concerns the other person and where they were struggling by considering their position.

Then, new information came to me, not only for his sake but also for mine. By taking his perspective, I noticed blind spots that my logic alone could not reveal.

Investment decisions ultimately must withstand criteria of facts, logic, risks, and returns. However, the other person's experiences and concerns are also part of the reality that should be included in judgment.

Our relationship of trust deepened. It became clear that I was trying to help him, and his perspective helped me.

> The perspective opened for the sake of the other person ultimately came to my own aid.

That was my greatest harvest.

## What I Thought Was "Myself"

I made another discovery.

For a long time, I had thought that my outward behavior—acting in an E-like manner by influencing others and leading them on the spot—was myself.

However, when I increased my time for solitary reflection through reading, meditation, and journaling, I noticed there was great joy and strength in it. The extroverted actions I acquired at work were not necessarily the same as my more natural tendencies.

If "I am an E" had been my final answer, I might not have been able to make the joys and strengths on the I side my own.

Here, the question of this book was born.

> Are those four letters words for understanding yourself? Or have they become words that rule out futures you have not yet tried?

## Mixing Four Things Turns Personality Into Destiny

The first distinction I want to make in this book is between the following four:

**Preference. Ability. Behavior. Identity.**

Thinking alone naturally might be a "preference."

However, that does not prove an "inability" to speak in public.

Not speaking at this meeting was a single instance of "behavior."

"I cannot speak in front of people." That is an "identity story" woven from that behavior.

When these four are mixed into one, "unnatural" becomes "cannot," and "did not do this time" becomes "this is who I really am."

At that moment, explanation turns into judgment.

## Most People Are Not Ruled by a Personality Test

I do not want to exaggerate the story here.

In a 2025 survey of 3,016 Japanese high school students, over 90% of girls and over 70% of boys answered that they had taken what is commonly called an “MBTI test.” On the other hand, when asked if they did not care about others’ MBTI types when interacting with people, over 90% of boys and over 80% of girls answered “yes.” Although there are limitations in the survey targets and methods, at least we cannot say that “people who took the test decide their relationships based on type.” [Note 1]

In another survey targeting university students, there is a significant difference between people who know their own type and those who consider it indispensable for their lives. [Note 2]

Many people enjoy using the four letters as conversation starters. Some do not take them seriously. Some can laugh at results changing with "well, that's just how it is."

What this book wants to address is not a binary choice of whether someone has taken the test or believes in it.

In which situations do the four letters help us, and in which situations do they exceed their role?

Have words we think we are enjoying infiltrated moments that decide our work, love, career path, or even our own possibilities?

We want to find that boundary together.

## Do Not Mix Three Things

I also want to make one important promise at the beginning.

In this book, Jung's type theory, official MBTI, and the four-letter type culture that spread on the internet are not treated as the same thing.

Many free tests called “MBTI” in Japan differ from the official MBTI assessment. In particular, 16Personalities, which is widely used, explains its own framework as a continuous trait model based on a reworking of the Big Five. Official MBTI practice, by contrast, includes a feedback process in which people examine the type that best fits them with a qualified practitioner. [Note 3]

Furthermore, official MBTI itself does not teach "it is okay to continue using only the one-sided aspect." Official type development theory emphasizes appropriately accessing processes other than preferences throughout life. The code of ethics also opposes using types to predict ability, intelligence, or success, or restricting a person's possibilities. [Note 4]

So, what is the problem?

The villain is not any particular company or testing site. Nor is it those of us who enjoy taking these tests.

The problem is that humans want to think of useful simplified diagrams as "themselves."

Maps are helpful for knowing one's current location. However, roads not drawn on the map do exist. The marker indicating the current location does not determine the destination.

The four letters are the same.

The four letters can explain you. They do not have to be your limitations.

## Try Changing One Question

The question I repeatedly use in this book is very simple.

Not "What type am I," but

**"What does this situation call for?"**

For example, a person who always prioritizes logic does not need to play the role of an emotional person. They can simply ask once what the person right in front of them values before reaching a conclusion.

A person who always thinks from possibilities does not need to become a person focused on details. They can simply write down three verifiable facts before moving a grand plan.

A person who prefers planning does not need to become unplanned. They can simply create margin to add new information to the initial plan.

A person who thinks alone does not need to become a social character. They can simply speak one prepared sentence in the first 10 minutes of a meeting.

Considering growth as becoming a new personality is too heavy.

If you choose just one different action next time, the door becomes slightly lighter.

## What This Book Promises and Does Not Promise

This book is not about changing your type.

It is not a book that makes E and I, S and N, T and F, J and P each half-half. It is not a book aiming to be an "all-purpose person" who can behave perfectly in any situation.

Preferences can remain. Strengths and weaknesses, as well as differences in energy, can remain.

What this book aims for is that when needed, one option other than your usual reaction becomes visible.

In other words, it is moving from "I am this kind of person" to "I can also do this."

To do so, we first look at how the four letters provide security and how they change into identity. Next, we return to Jung's type theory's original focus on "one-sidedness of the mind." Then, we repurpose the four pairs of letters not as boxes that determine personality but as entrances to verify omissions.

At the end of each chapter, there is a small experiment.

In the latter half, we summarize that experiment into one procedure.

**Notice. Reconsider. Try Small. Reweave.**

These are not the Four Steps to Change Your Personality. They are the "Four-Step Choice Practice" for inserting one choice between automatic reactions and reality.

You do not need to change greatly. You do not even need to believe. Try it, observe it, and keep only what is useful.

Self-understanding does not end when you can explain yourself.

I think it truly begins when that understanding allows you to choose one more thing than yesterday.

Protecting oneself and confining oneself sometimes happen with the same words.

Let's start walking beyond the four letters by distinguishing that difference.
