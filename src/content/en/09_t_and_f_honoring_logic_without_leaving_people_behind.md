# Chapter 9: T and F — Honoring Logic Without Leaving People Behind

"I didn't want a solution. I just wanted to be heard."

Have you ever felt puzzled by hearing these words?

There is a problem. You have organized the causes, presented options, and offered a good solution. Yet, the other person appears even more hurt.

There are also opposite scenarios.

No one wants to hurt anyone, so conclusions remain vague. As everyone's circumstances are heard, the criteria shift. In the end, only you take on the burden, and dissatisfaction accumulates in your heart.

The power to uphold principles and the power not to leave people behind.

Thinking (T) and Feeling (F) serve as an entry point for considering what is given weight when making a judgment.

This does not mean Thinking is "smart" and Feeling is "emotional." In official terminology, both are processes of judgment.

Judgment that prioritizes consistent principles and causality versus judgment that prioritizes impact on people and values.

Good decisions sometimes require both.

## From T to F—When the Right Answer Is Not Received

The power to organize logic is helpful in chaos.

It separates fact from opinion, finds contradictions, and applies the same criteria. Even in emotionally charged situations, it considers what is the cause and what is the result.

However, in problems involving people, a decision based solely on logical correctness does not always work.

What does the other person lose? What value do they feel has been trampled? Are they in a state where they can receive an explanation? Do they feel they participated in the decision?

These are not the opposite of logic.

They are information necessary for a decision to move forward in reality.

In the dialogue with my investment partner discussed in the Prologue, this was also information I overlooked.

At that time, I thought that if I explained the correct logic more strongly, the other person would agree. However, there were facts not included in my judgment until I asked what the other person was seeing.

From this experience, I came to think as follows:

> Questions from the F side bring information within the relationship into the judgment. Criteria from the T side put the decision in a form that can withstand verification.

Neither is ranked higher than the other. They connect different types of information and responsibilities within a single decision.

Here, we are often told to "try to see things from the other person's perspective."

However, imagination alone may not accurately understand the other person.

Eyal, Steffel, and Epley investigated in 25 experiments whether instructing people to take another's perspective consistently improves judgment accuracy regarding others' emotions, preferences, and attitudes. While participants' effort and confidence increased, evidence of consistently improved accuracy was not obtained. In the final experiment, accuracy improved when the other person's perspective was obtained directly through conversation.[Note 1]

Simply reasoning well about another person's mind in your head is not enough.

**Imagining another person’s perspective is not the same as receiving it.**

### The Smallest Step: Listen, Summarize, and Confirm

Before reaching a conclusion, do three things.

1.  **Listen:** "What is most important to you regarding this matter?"
2.  **Summarize:** "So, my understanding is that the result matters less than being decided without consultation; is that correct?"
3.  **Confirm:** "Do you want to think about a solution together now? Or do you just want to be heard for a bit more?"

Empathy does not become an emotional talent.

It becomes an action of acquiring information and confirming understanding.

A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials on empathy training also reports that the training is effective overall. However, the training content and measurement methods vary, and there are limits in how they translate into long-term real-world actions.[Note 2]

Therefore, we check until the other person confirms that they feel "understood."

## From F to T—Making Kindness Sustainable

The power that prioritizes impact on people finds those left out of a decision.

Even with the same rules, the burden differs depending on the conditions placed upon them. Even with a correct explanation, the way it is conveyed can prevent the other person from participating. There is trust and dignity that cannot be expressed in numbers.

However, trying to respond to all feelings simultaneously creates another kind of unfairness.

Only the requests of loud voices get through. Work accumulates for those who do not refuse. The vagueness intended to avoid hurting others leaves long-term anxiety.

Stating criteria is not coldness.

It is making it possible to explain to whom, why, and under what conditions the same judgment is made.

### The Smallest Step: Write Down Three Criteria Before Deciding

In decisions likely to be swayed by people's requests, write down the criteria first.

For example, if considering taking on new work:

*   Can I maintain my current responsibilities?
*   Does this work align with my or my team's purpose?
*   Do I have the necessary time and authority?

Look at each one. If exceptions are allowed, write down the reasons as well.

This is not about excluding emotions like "that person is pitiful."

It places impact on people on the same table as other conditions.

With criteria in place, the words for refusing also change.

Instead of "I can't," say:

"I cannot take this on right now because I won't be able to keep my Friday commitment. If we need to change priorities, I want to decide together which one to stop."

Upholding principles can sometimes protect relationships.

Ai in Chapter 3 learned this from the opposite direction.

When asked for help by a colleague, the reason they are in trouble becomes very visible. Thinking that refusing would disappoint them, she accepts. As a result, work promised earlier is delayed, and Ai ends up staying late alone. Despite her consideration, resentment remains afterward.

Before answering the next request immediately, she checks three criteria: Can I keep my current commitments? Is this something I need to take on myself? Does the person with the authority to change priorities agree to the change?

Then, she conveys: "I want to help. However, if I take this on now, I cannot keep my previous commitment. If we need to change priorities, I want to decide with my supervisor included."

The other person looks a bit troubled. Not everything is resolved perfectly. Nevertheless, the boundaries for what needs protection become clear. Instead of holding onto dissatisfaction while continuing to help, Ai gains the choice to adjust within the relationship.

## Empathy and Agreement Are Different

When practicing the power on the F side, some people feel that "once I understand the other person, I must accept their wishes."

Understanding and agreement are separate.

"I understand the reason you were hurt."

After that, you can say:

"Nevertheless, I cannot comply with this request."

Conversely, when using the power on the T side, one might think "if the criteria are the same, it is fair."

However, if starting conditions differ, the same treatment may not produce the same result. Recognizing no exceptions does not always mean fairness.

Therefore, we list two questions side by side.

"Can this judgment be explained to others?"
"What impact will this judgment have on whom?"

Principles and impacts check each other.

## Do Not Treat Emotions as "Noise" or "Commands"

Emotions can distort judgment.

Anger makes dangers seem larger. Favoritism leads to evaluating only liked people highly. Anxiety causes small possibilities to feel like certain failures.

However, eliminating emotions does not mean the judgment becomes correct.

Emotions are also information indicating that values and needs are involved.

Anger indicates a boundary has been violated. Sadness indicates something has been lost. Fear indicates there is something to protect.

Information is not a command.

Just because you are angry does not mean you must attack. Just because you are afraid does not mean you must flee.

Receive emotions as one piece of data and judge them together with facts, criteria, and impacts.

The integration of T and F is not taking a middle ground between calmness and emotion.

It is putting what is considered correct and who experiences what into the same decision.

## Small Experiment: Turn One Guess into a Question

Now, write down one guess you hold about someone.

> That person must be thinking that I am ________.

Next, change it into a question safe to ask the person themselves.

> I received ________ from you. What was most important to you? Was it ________? Or something else?

After hearing the answer, do not immediately add your own explanation. Instead, summarize once and confirm.

Finally, convey one of your own judgment criteria.

Receiving the other's perspective and not losing your own criteria.

Do not choose just one; do both in sequence.

Judgments where logic holds and people remain are born from this back-and-forth.
