# Chapter 7: E and I — The Power to Connect and the Power to Step Back

In Chapter 3, Makoto always comes up with the best words after a meeting ends.

"I should have said that."

On the way home on the train, her reasoning is clear and logical. But during the meeting, while listening to others' voices, she cannot organize her own thoughts. As a young manager, her opinions ultimately do not make it into the team's decision.

Conversely, there are people who can produce words one after another in a discussion setting but feel anxious when alone.

"Since everyone agreed, this must be fine."

They were certain at the time. But by the next morning, they no longer know if they truly felt convinced.

If we view E and I as classifications of sociable versus shy people, these two scenarios end as "personality differences." [Note 1]

However, when viewed as entry points for action, two distinct capacities become visible.

The capacity to connect with the outside, receive information, and act upon it.

The capacity to step back slightly from the outside, deepen thoughts internally, and listen to one's own judgment.

Neither is superior.

The issue is whether the capacity currently needed by the situation aligns with the capacity the self automatically selects.

## E Is Not "Popular," I Is Not "Shy"

In everyday four-letter culture, E is often described as bright and talkative, while I is described as quiet and bad at socializing.

In reality, sociability, assertiveness, preference for stimulation, methods of organizing thoughts, and ways of recovering are not entirely the same.

People who lean toward I may speak well with familiar partners. If the purpose is clear, they can handle sales or public speaking. People who lean toward E may feel nervous in unfamiliar groups. Even if one enjoys meeting people, time to think deeply is necessary.

It is also important not to explain social anxiety or shyness solely through the letter I.

If we think, "Because you are introverted, you should endure fear and step into the spotlight," we might overlook necessary support. If we think, "There is no need to fix it because you are introverted," we might solidify the person's suffering as a fixed personality trait.

We look at the person's difficulties, hopes, and bodily reactions—not just the letter.

## From I to E—Stepping Outside Brings Information Back

Thinking alone has significant strengths.

One does not have to match others' pace; complex thoughts can be nurtured. One can choose one's own words without reacting immediately. One can detach from the heat of the group and question assumptions.

However, if one does not bring thoughts outside, there is information that cannot be obtained.

Where the other party might misunderstand. What is missing in one's explanation. What is happening on the ground. Who can be helped.

Even a perfect plan in the head reveals gaps the moment it is spoken aloud.

Engaging with the outside world is not just about self-expression.

It is a method of receiving feedback from reality.

### First Step: Speak One Sentence in the First Half of the Meeting

Telling someone who cannot speak up in meetings to "try to be more proactive" is not helpful.

We make the action small.

1. Before the meeting, write down one observation you most want to convey in a single sentence.
2. Do not complete a counterargument; instead, frame it as a question or a fact.
3. Speak that one sentence within the first 10 minutes of the meeting.

If waiting for an opportunity to speak will cause delay, use an if-then plan. [Note 2]

> If the moderator asks, "Is there anything?" during the first agenda item, read the prepared sentence.

The criterion for success is not speaking well.

It is whether new information came back because something was brought outside.

Makoto read the materials the day before the meeting and wrote one sentence: "It appears that an estimate of the on-site burden has not been included." On the day, she read it within the first 10 minutes.

Her voice was quiet, and her explanation was not perfect. However, another member checked the numbers, and part of the plan was revised. What she gained was not a new identity as "someone who can speak well." It was the experience that even with a prepared sentence, one can bring necessary information outside to support a decision.

## From E to I—Separating Reaction Speed from One's Own Answer

People for whom connecting with the outside is natural move situations forward.

They think while speaking, choosing their next words based on the other party's reactions. They advance stalled meetings. They call out to those who need help. They learn by trying.

However, the more external reactions there are, the less visible one's own judgment may become.

One feels convinced because the other party is convinced. As one speaks, the easiest thought to express becomes the most correct thought. Silence feels anxious, leading one to decide even on matters that do not yet need a decision.

Time spent returning inward is not isolation.

It is time to pass information gained from the outside through one's own values and judgment.

### First Step: Step Away for Just 10 Minutes Before an Important Reply

If replying immediately has become a habit, insert a short pause only for important requests or decisions.

> "I will organize my thoughts and reply by 3 PM today."

Once alone, write down three things:

- What do I agree with?
- What feels off to me?
- If I cannot see anyone else's reactions, what do I choose?

Ten minutes is sufficient; deep introspection is not always necessary.

For those who turn overthinking into rumination, set a time limit and write down one next action at the end.

Introversion does not guarantee depth, and extroversion does not mean shallowness.

Both can become strengths or escape routes depending on how they are used.

## Prepare Before Connecting, Then Connect After Preparing

There is a way to use E and I without treating them as an either/or choice.

"Prepare alone before speaking to people."

"Decide alone after gathering information by speaking to people."

We combine the two in sequence.

People who lean toward I do not need to jump into extroverted situations without preparation. They can take one step outside using the strength of preparation.

People who lean toward E do not need to stop interacting with others. They can turn materials gathered outside into their own answers through brief introspection.

The capacity on the opposite side does not necessarily negate current strengths; it may complete them.

Makoto did not become someone who immediately joined all after-meeting meals. She still prepares alone and recovers alone. However, she now steps outside once, just enough to let her prepared thoughts touch reality.

## Small Experiment: Increase the Round-Trip of Information by One

Choose one recent problem where your thinking has stalled.

### If You Are Not Stepping Outside

Speak your "current hypothesis," not a conclusion, to someone and receive one question.

### If You Are Not Returning Inward

Step away for 10 minutes after conversations or meetings and write down the distinction between others' opinions and your own judgment.

Recording this is not about whether your personality has changed.

- Was new information obtained?
- Did the judgment become clearer?
- How much was depleted, and what was needed to recover?

The power to connect and the power to reflect alone.

With both, thoughts touch reality while becoming one's own.
