# Chapter 3: How a Label Becomes ‘Me’

The first time I read my test results, I simply thought, “This fits.”

Soon after, I began seeing posts on social media featuring similar types.

*"INFPs can't reply in situations like this."*
*"Things ENTJs dislike at work."*
*"What happens when J and P go traveling together."*

I would save them with a laugh and send them to friends. In return, they would send me messages saying, "This is exactly you."

Eventually, I started saying it myself:
*"I'm really an INFP."*

Here, the role of the four letters shifts slightly.

At first, they were labels used to explain past experiences. Now, they have become "me," a tool for predicting future experiences.

How does a label transform into identity?

One experiment cannot prove everything. However, by reviewing research on fixed thinking about personality, feedback from psychological tests, essentialism, and the repetition of daily life, the path becomes clear.

## Drawing Boundaries Across Continuous Differences

As seen in the previous chapter, most personality traits are continuous.

Using extraversion as an example, there are not only "complete extraverts" and "complete introverts" in the world; people are distributed across various positions and change their behavior depending on the situation.

However, continuous numbers are difficult to handle.

Saying "My extraversion is slightly lower than the average of the population, with some fluctuation based on the situation" is slower than simply saying, "I am an I." It is easier to remember and easier to find peers.

Categories compress complexity.

Compression itself is not necessarily bad. We live our lives by naming various continuities such as color, sound, illness, profession, and generation. There are things we cannot discuss without names.

However, boundaries have side effects.

49 and 51 are close. Yet, if divided by a line on either side, they appear to be different kinds.

51 and 90 are far apart. Yet, if placed on the same side, they appear to be the same kind.

Furthermore, when figures such as "Advocate," "Commander," or "Mediator" are attached to categories, numerical differences transform into narrative differences.

## People Tend to View Personality as Something "Inside"

In psychology, viewing a certain trait as an "essence"—innate, difficult to change, and consistently appearing across various situations—is sometimes called essentialism.

Research by Haslam et al. showed that people tend to view personality traits as having "clear boundaries," being "innate and hard to change," and representing an "essence behind behavior." Furthermore, traits prone to essentialization tend to be placed at the center of a person's identity and others' judgments.[1]

An explanation of behavior such as,
*"This person gave a cold response this time,"*

transforms into an essential explanation like,
*"This person is a T and inherently lacks empathy."*

Essential explanations are easy to understand; they allow us to stop searching for causes.

However, they eliminate the situation.

There was no time. I didn't know what to say. I misunderstood the other person's wishes. I was also hurt. The role required a harsh judgment.

There are many conditions that produce the same behavior. Essentialism summarizes this complexity into a single internal cause.

## Viewing Personality as Fixed Changes Perception

People who believe personality is fixed and those who believe it can change differ in how they view others.

Levy, Stroessner, and Dweck examined five studies on how "entity theory," which views personality as fixed, and "incremental theory," which views it as developable, relate to the formation and support of stereotypes.[2] People holding a fixed view of personality were found to be more likely to summarize characteristics about social groups and stereotype them. The same directional effect was observed even when experiments temporarily prompted a fixed perspective.[3]

This research did not examine MBTI specifically.

Nevertheless, it teaches us where to be careful in four-letter culture.

When we read "This type is like this," we are not merely acquiring information; we may be practicing the very view that people are divided into fixed kinds.

We then apply this view to ourselves:
*"I am this kind of person, so I won't change."*
*"If I were to change, it wouldn't be my true self."*

Thus, counterexamples to behavior are recovered not as evidence of growth, but as explanations such as "pushing myself too hard" or "wearing a mask."

## Feedback Can Influence Subsequent Behavior

Does a test result merely explain the past?

There is an interesting small-scale experiment conducted in Japan.

In 2000, sixty-four female university students were given feedback labeled as either "extraverted" or "introverted," which was actually manipulated to resemble personality test results. In subsequent interactions, behavioral changes aligned with the given feedback were observed. This effect occurred whether the test was presented as a general-purpose psychological test or an academic examination.[4]

The sample was small, consisting only of female university students, and the research is dated. It did not use MBTI, nor does it demonstrate long-term personality change.

Nevertheless, it shows that a personality test does not always neutrally reflect a personality that already exists.

After receiving results, people may choose actions that fit the results and pay attention to memories that align with them.

Even if a personality test is a mirror, the person looking into it adjusts their hair.

The mirror and the subject are not completely independent.

## Only What Is Confirmed Remains

After knowing one's type, daily life becomes a place for gathering evidence.

Returning tired from a large dinner party:
*"I'm an I after all."*

Making detailed travel plans:
*"I'm too much of a J."*

Offering solutions to a friend's consultation:
*"It's unavoidable because I'm a T."*

Events that match predictions are immediately linked to the type.

What about events that do not match?

On days when we had fun talking with a stranger for the first time, it was "because our hobbies were the same." On days when all plans were changed, it was "because of an emergency." On days where we listened to a crying friend until the end, it was "because that person is special."

Situational explanations may all be correct.

However, if only actions that fit are treated as evidence of the type and all non-fitting actions are dismissed as exceptions, the type becomes infallible.

An explanation with no possibility of error is comforting but cannot be used for learning.

Because the same conclusion is reached regardless of the outcome.

## Four Moments When Labels Narrow Behavior

Whether four letters have crossed their role boundary is distinguished not by the strength of belief, but by usage.

### 1. Becoming a Get-Out-of-Jail-Free Card
*"I'm a P, so I'm late."*
*"I'm a T, so my tone is harsh."*

Accepting no responsibility for the impact of actions and ending with an explanation based on type.

What can be explained and what disappears as responsibility are separate matters.

### 2. Becoming a Prophecy
*"I can't do sales because I'm an I."*
*"Planning isn't suitable for me because I'm an S."*

Deciding the result before experiencing it and losing opportunities to try.

If one does not try, one does not fail, but information to correct prophecies is also not obtained.

### 3. Becoming a Verdict on Others
*"F types can't speak logically."*
*"I can't travel with J types."*

Inferring ability, intent, and compatibility from four letters before asking the person themselves.

### 4. Becoming a Filter for Opportunities

In hiring, assignment, leader selection, and choosing romantic partners, type is prioritized over actual experience or skills.

This goes beyond self-understanding of the individual and influences opportunities for others. This is why official MBTI ethics oppose using it for hiring, selection, or ability inference.

## What Three People Lost Within Labels

From here on, we follow three composite cases.

Maki, a young manager, excels when thinking alone and preparing. Saying "I'm an I" allowed her to decline tiring dinner parties. However, with the same words, she began declining opportunities to express opinions in meetings as well. Words meant to protect rest had become words that relinquished influence.

Yuto, who creates new services, believed, *"Not solidifying ideas is my strength because I'm an N/P."* Every time he changed direction, possibilities increased, but he could never present something to users.

Ayumi, trusted at work, was someone who read people's expectations and kept promises. She spoke of herself as a "person like F/J, with strong responsibility." Because of this, she could not decline requests and, to keep the work she accepted, put aside existing promises and her own recovery.

None of the three were completely wrong about their labels. The problem was that words explaining strengths began automatically deciding subsequent actions.

## You Don't Have to Abandon Labels Yet

Reading up to here might make you think, "Labels are all dangerous; it's better not to use them."

However, removing names does not eliminate one-sidedness.

Even without knowing a type, people categorize themselves as "sunshine person," "shady person," "capable worker," or "sensitive person." They explain themselves as "I've always been like this."

The problem is not just the existence of labels.

Can we hold labels as provisional explanations? Can we correct them with counterexamples? Do we avoid using them before asking questions of the person themselves? Do we not turn them into commands for the future?

There are three rephrasings to hold labels lightly.

Instead of "I am an I," say, "For now, an explanation leaning toward I fits well."

Instead of "I can't speak in front of people," say, "Speaking in front of people is draining for me; preparation is needed."

Instead of "That person is cold because they're a T," say, "I felt that way of speaking was cold. I haven't yet confirmed the other person's intent."

Return nouns to degree, condition, and action.

Then, labels return from being "me itself" to being "one hypothesis when observing me."

## Small Experiment: Return Three Counterexamples to the Story

Choose one explanation about your type that you believe most strongly.

> I am a person who is ________.

Next, find three events that did not fit this description.

- When, where, and with whom.
- What was actually done.
- What made that action possible.

You do not need to deny the "true type" using counterexamples.

Finally, combine two into one sentence.

> I usually choose ________. At the same time, under conditions of ________, I can also ________.

This "at the same time" creates space in identity.

It is okay not to worry about being inconsistent.

In living life, being able to respond to different situations in reality is more useful than fitting into one label without contradiction.
