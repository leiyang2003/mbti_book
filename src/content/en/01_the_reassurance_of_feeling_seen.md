# Chapter 1: The Reassurance of Feeling Seen

Have you ever read a personality assessment result and felt compelled to laugh?

"It's uncannily accurate."

"Did someone look at me and write this?"

The screen displays your strengths, weaknesses, social habits, and common relationship issues. As you read, the fragmented pieces of your past begin to coalesce into a single narrative.

That time you stayed silent in a large group. That time you became angry over sudden schedule changes. That time you got caught up in a detail no one else seemed to notice. That time you explained a solution while the other person was crying.

"I see. It all connects."

It is easy to dismiss this feeling as mere projection. However, there is something worth considering before doing so.

Why do we feel such a profound need for short phrases that explain ourselves?

## We Cannot Explain Ourselves Well Enough

We spend our days in the company of ourselves. Yet, explaining who we are proves surprisingly difficult.

When asked in an interview, "What are your strengths?" you may find yourself at a loss for words.

When a partner asks, "Why don't you say anything?" you may not even understand why yourself.

When a colleague says, "You don't need to plan that meticulously," you may struggle to explain why planning is necessary in a way they can understand.

Inside ourselves, sensations, memories, physical fatigue, expectations, and anxieties become entangled. These are things that have not yet taken shape as words.

Then come the four letters:

I. N. F. P.

Just four symbols outline complex experiences. Moreover, descriptions from others who share the same letters or "this happens to me" moments are found immediately.

"I was not alone."

This sense of reassurance is genuine.

Even if every detail of the assessment is not scientifically accurate, the reassurance does not become fake. If a certain phrase reduces the need to blame oneself slightly, that experience holds value.

## Turning Weaknesses into Differences

One of the greatest gifts provided by type language is transforming moral issues into matters of difference.

Someone who thinks while talking may perceive someone who thinks silently as "slow to react." Conversely, someone who thinks silently may view someone who talks while thinking as "speaking on a whim."

Someone who wants to decide quickly may see someone who keeps options open as "indecisive." Someone who keeps options open may view someone who decides quickly as "inflexible."

Someone who prioritizes facts and consistency may call someone who considers the impact on relationships first "emotional." Someone who considers the impact on relationships first may call someone who prioritizes facts "cold."

When we cannot articulate these differences, we tend to interpret others' personalities negatively.

The type framework places an alternative explanation there.

"It is not a lack of ability; perhaps they simply direct their attention elsewhere first."

"It is not that they are trying to trouble me; perhaps their order of judgment is different."

This "perhaps" creates space in conversation.

Four letters simplify people. However, meeting four letters can sometimes prevent us from viewing others through simple binaries of good and evil, as before encountering them. Here lies the contradiction of type language.

Simplification can lead to shallow understanding or serve as a foothold for beginning understanding.

## Why Four Letters Spread in Japan

In Japan, so-called MBTI assessments or 16-type diagnostics have become a common language, particularly among younger generations.

A survey conducted by the Tokyo Advertising Association in 2025 targeting four-year university students found that 46.1% answered that they know their MBTI type or hold an opinion about it.[Note 1] While the sample is limited and cannot be generalized to all of Japan, this indicates a foothold for four letters in young people's conversations.

The reason for their spread is not solely because the assessments "fit."

First, they are easy to participate in. One answers on a smartphone and receives results immediately. Even without knowing specialized terminology, one can understand through character descriptions and brief explanations.

Second, they are easy to share. Without recounting a long life history, one can write their four letters in a profile. A single question—"What type are you?"—can initiate conversation.

Third, they are easy to compare. One can list the types of oneself, friends, partners, and favorite idols to discuss similarities and differences.

Fourth, they make self-analysis easier. The question "Who are you?" is heavy. However, "Are you more inclined toward I or E?" seems answerable.

Fast, light, and shareable. This is a strong format in environments where time and attention compete fiercely.

However, knowing something quickly is not the same as understanding it deeply.

## Conversation Starters and Conversation Endings

Four letters work most effectively when placed at the beginning of a conversation.

"I am an I. In what situations do you prefer to be alone?"

"You're a J. When schedules change suddenly, what troubles you most?"

Questions follow the letters. Then, types become an entry point for knowing people.

Conversely, when four letters are placed at the end of a conversation, their function changes.

"I'm an I. So, I'm not good at situations like this, right?"

"You're a T, so don't expect empathy."

"If you're a P, you'll be late for appointments."

Questions disappear, leaving only predictions.

What is crucial here is that these predictions can sometimes come true.

Indeed, one might struggle in large groups. Indeed, one might prioritize problem-solving over the other person's feelings. Every time an assessment result aligns with behavior, one feels, "Types are indeed correct."

However, alignment does not prove that a person always acts that way. Nor does it prove that they cannot learn different behaviors in the future.

There is a significant distance between "often happens" and "always happens."

As seen in the introduction, most people do not determine their human relationships based on their type.[Note 2] The issue is not the strength of belief, but whether four letters provide an answer before one's own experience in anxious choice situations.

## Stopping at "I Understand": What Remains

When we become able to explain ourselves, our emotions settle.

However, explanation and change are separate.

"I have a tendency to prioritize solutions over the other person's feelings."

We understand that much. Yet, the same arguments with our partner continue.

"I start late when given flexible deadlines."

We understand that too. Yet, we will be late again next month.

"I overthink alone and take external information slowly."

The explanation has become accurate. Yet, choices have not increased.

Here lies the line this book aims to cross.

Self-understanding cannot be measured solely by whether the explanation fits.

Does this understanding reduce self-blame? Does it increase questions directed toward others? Can we change reactions that do not match reality? Do options previously invisible become visible?

When "I understand" transforms into "I can choose," self-understanding becomes powerful.

In the next chapter, we will make the initial organization necessary for this.

What we collectively call "MBTI" is actually not one thing. There is Jung's type theory, official MBTI, and 16-type culture on the internet. Knowing the differences between each reveals how much reliance we can place on four letters.

## Small Record: Three Things Four Letters Gave You

Before beginning criticism, please record what four letters have given you.

1. No longer needing to blame yourself.
2. Finding it easier to understand differences with someone.
3. Finding it easier to start conversations and actions.

It is fine if you do not have all three. Even having none is an important answer.

Next, consider just one scene where this help went too far.

> Because of this phrase, I understood. But because of this phrase, I decided without confirming __________.

Before discarding a tool, know what it helped with.

That is the first step toward not being used by the tool but reusing it.
