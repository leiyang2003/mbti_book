# Chapter 10: J and P — The Power to Decide and the Power to Stay Open

On a morning trip, the train stopped.

One person immediately looked up a new route and recalculated the arrival time.

The other said, "Since we're here, let's look around this area before deciding."

To the first person, it seems the other does not understand the seriousness of the situation. To the second person, it seems the first is so obsessed with keeping to the schedule that they are not enjoying the journey itself.

The power to decide and the power to stay open.

J and P are an entry point for considering this tension between the two.

This is not a moral classification of J as neat and P as sloppy.

Furthermore, Jung did not present J/P as an independent fourth axis; Myers and Briggs added these indicators explicitly. Here, we borrow from that theory not to treat the whole system as proven, but to examine everyday actions of convergence and openness.[2]

## From J to P — Protecting the Purpose Rather Than the Plan

The power to decide quickly advances action.

Set deadlines, define roles, and close off unfinished items. By reducing options, you concentrate attention and time.

Decisions have value.

If nothing is decided, possibilities remain, but reality does not move.

However, plans are based on information available at the time they were made.

When new facts emerge, changes may feel like failures. Because time has already been invested in the plan, you stick to it even if it no longer aligns with your purpose. You become angry about unexpected events and lose sight of usable information.

Plans serve the purpose, not the other way around.

The power on the P side is not about irresponsibility.

It is the ability to update methods based on information returned from reality.

### First Step: Divide Decisions into "Trial Parts" and "Finalized Parts"

Deciding everything at once makes changes heavy.

Divide decisions into two stages.

- Decide tentatively now.
- Determine when and what information will be used for the final decision.

For a new meeting method, do not roll it out company-wide; try it with one team for two weeks. For changing jobs, before resigning, talk to people currently working there and get a feel for the work through small side projects or courses.

Also write down the conditions for change in advance.

> If two out of three users stop at the same location, change the procedure.

Make flexibility an update based on information, not a change driven by mood.

## From P to J — Having Options Is Different From Not Choosing

The power to keep open gives strength against change.

Take in new information and seize unexpected opportunities. Do not cling to the first plan; be able to shift to a better method. Find value that was not part of the plan through play or chance.

However, options have no end.

The more you research, the more other possibilities appear. You feel there might be an even better option. At the moment of decision, you lose the futures you did not choose.

Not deciding also has results.

Application deadlines pass. The other party stops waiting. Unfinished work continues to consume attention in your mind.

Undecided matters that protect freedom can sometimes reduce the number of future choices available to you.

Yuto from Chapter 8 still faced the following problem: he obtained information from paper prototypes. However, trying to reflect everything he noticed led to another concept expanding, making the next version impossible.

What Yuto needed was not a personality that dislikes ideas, but an action that closes off the scope for moving into reality next.

The power on the J side is not about hating possibilities.

It is the power to close off other possibilities once in order to bring important possibilities into reality.

### First Step: Decide on the Minimum Version That Can Be Shown

If you aim for perfect completion, both starting and finishing become heavy.

First define "the version that can be shown to someone at a minimum."

- For a proposal document: purpose, target audience, and the first step.
- For a manuscript: a rough draft of 1,000 words.
- For tidying a room: just the desk surface.

Then make the start an if-then rule.

> If I put my morning coffee on the desk, then I will create the minimum version for only 15 minutes.

You may stop after 15 minutes.

Success is not about degree of completion, but that something visible has been created.

Visible things receive feedback; possibilities in your head do not.

Yuto decided the minimum version was "a version where two users can complete the first task." He did not discard ideas that could not fit into that version, but moved them to notes for the next iteration.

The published minimum version was much smaller and less complete than what he had imagined in his head. However, he did not lose a possibility; he turned one possibility into a form where he could learn from reality.

## Uncertainty Cannot Be Reduced to Zero

Behind the strengthening of J-leaning actions may lie discomfort with uncertainty.

If plans are set, you feel secure. If conclusions are reached, you do not need to keep thinking.

P-leaning actions also have another reaction to uncertainty.

If you do not decide, you do not confirm that you made a wrong choice. By keeping options open, you can postpone anxious evaluations.

Deciding quickly and not deciding can both become ways to avoid uncertainty.

In reality, only a few problems allow you to decide after gathering all the information.

What is needed is not to like uncertainty.

It is distinguishing what you can proceed with despite unknowns from what you should stop if something remains unknown.

### Three Columns Before Deciding

- What is known.
- What is unknown.
- What you can do now even while things remain unknown.

Transform uncertainty from a huge fog into named blank spaces.

Research on psychological interventions targeting uncertainty has mainly reported improvements in clinical groups related to anxiety. However, the J/P practice in this book cannot be treated as therapy. If strong anxiety interferes with daily life, do not attribute it to type issues; consider seeking professional support.[1]

## Place Plans and Margins on the Same Calendar

Plans and freedom appear opposite.

However, margins can be planned.

Insert two hours of doing nothing into your itinerary. Set a review date for projects. At the end of meetings, clarify points that will not be decided.

Conversely, to protect freedom, you must decide on several things.

Decide how much money you can use, when you will return home, and what promises to keep; within that range, you can feel secure enough to take detours.

Combining J and P is not about setting only half the schedule.

It is consciously deciding what to close off and what to keep open.

## Small Experiment: Tentative Decisions and Minimum Versions

Choose one problem where you are currently deciding too much or unable to decide at all.

### If You Are Deciding Too Much

1. Write down the true purpose.
2. Choose one part that can still be changed.
3. Decide the date to consider changes and the information to observe.

### If You Cannot Decide

1. Define the minimum version that can be shown.
2. Set a 15-minute starting line with an if-then rule.
3. Show what you made to one person.

Finally, record the following:

- What was lost by deciding.
- What was gained by deciding.
- Information gained by keeping open.
- Cost paid by keeping open.

Both deciding and opening have a cost.

Maturity is not about making the cost zero.

In this situation, it is about choosing which cost to accept.
